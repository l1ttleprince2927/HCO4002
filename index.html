Part 2 – Reflective Writing

1. What I learned and how my understanding of AI evolved

I came into this course with a fairly clear view of AI, and that view has not really changed: AI is powerful, but it is still a tool. Much of the content was already familiar to me. In earlier courses I studied probability and statistics, data structures and data mining, and machine learning, and I built a pipeline that predicted Hong Kong immigration traffic using XGBoost, Random Forest and Linear Regression. Topics such as transformers, fine-tuning and model evaluation were therefore reviews rather than new ideas.

What the course added was breadth rather than a change of belief. In a few weeks we moved from foundation models to design thinking, full-stack and multimodal tools, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), LoRA fine-tuning, agentic platforms and generative media. Seeing these tools side by side made it easier to judge what each one is good for. Gemini Canvas is fast for building an interface, Dify is designed for knowledge-based chat and workflows, and Colab suits data processing in Python. One distinction I found useful to revisit was RAG versus fine-tuning: RAG changes what a model can look up, while LoRA fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. RAG therefore suits content that changes often, such as a curriculum or a daily list of surplus food.

The group project confirmed my original view rather than challenging it. Gemini Canvas produced a polished, working interface for our food-rescue app, FoodMatch HK, very quickly. But when we checked the code, the “AI assistant” returned the same fixed reply to every question, and the merchant “AI parsing” filled most fields with default values. The tool generated something that looked convincing; deciding whether it actually did what we claimed was still our job. For me, this is the clearest example of AI as a tool: it speeds up the work, but responsibility for the result stays with the person using it.

2. The most challenging concepts or tools, and why

To be honest, no single concept in the course was technically hard for me, because most of them overlapped with my previous study. The challenges I met were practical rather than conceptual.

The first was describing a prototype accurately. I was responsible for the technical part of our group proposal: the practical approach, the prototype features and the live demo. Our draft described semantic matching, AI parsing and a carbon calculation based on category-specific emission factors, while the prototype itself used scripted replies and preset numbers. Writing a description that was honest but still explained the intended AI design took more care than any concept in the lessons. A reader cannot see the difference between “planned” and “built” unless the writer makes it clear.

The second was choosing a platform under real constraints. In theory, Dify, Base44, Gemini Canvas and Colab can all support a prototype. In practice, our choice depended on time, on who in the group could edit the site, and on what would run reliably in a live demo. The course comparisons helped, but the decision still came down to trade-offs rather than one right answer.

The third was checking sources. When we added references to our proposal, one framework named in our draft could not be found under that name, and one claim about Dify did not match its official documentation. This reminded me that AI-assisted writing makes it easy to produce confident text, and that checking each claim against a real source is a separate step that cannot be skipped.

Finally, preparing the live demo was a challenge of its own. Because some features were scripted, I planned the demo around the paths that worked and prepared a direct answer if someone asked whether the AI was real. Being open about the prototype’s limits felt safer than hoping nobody would notice them.

3. How my perspective on responsible AI use has developed

My basic position stayed the same: AI should support human judgement, not replace it. What developed was a more concrete sense of what responsible use means in a real product.

In FoodMatch HK, the risks were specific. If an AI model guessed the allergens in a merchant’s post, a resident could be harmed, so allergen information should come only from what the merchant states, and the app should say “not stated” otherwise. Location is personal data, so the app shows the district rather than a street address. Food safety also needs a human check: a merchant must confirm that the food has been stored safely before a listing goes live. None of these safeguards is technically difficult, but each depends on someone deciding in advance where AI should not be trusted.

Generative media raises different questions. For my Lesson 6 artefact I generated images from text prompts. As that artefact shows, my first image placed a Hong Kong topic in a UK-style classroom, and even the refined version still contained meaningless symbols, so generated images need checking before use. AI images can also misrepresent people, imitate recognisable styles or mislead viewers if they are not labelled. I now think responsible use includes stating clearly which content is AI-generated and keeping the prompts, so that others can see how it was produced.

Finally, responsible use applies to my own work. Generative AI was permitted in this course, and I used it for drafting and checking. I see disclosure as part of academic honesty rather than a formality: readers should know which parts were assisted, and I remain responsible for every claim.

4. Connections to my major field: ICT Education

As a future secondary school ICT teacher, I see this course mainly through one question: how will my students use AI? My view that AI is a tool shapes what I want to teach. Students should learn to use AI to work faster, but also to check its output, cite real sources and say when they have used it. The gap between “the prototype looks like it uses AI” and “the prototype actually uses AI” is exactly the kind of critical thinking that ICT lessons should build.

Several course topics connect directly to teaching. Design thinking helps me plan lessons around students’ real difficulties rather than around the textbook alone, and an empathy map of DSE ICT students is a quick way to start. RAG is the basis of my Part 3 proposal: a revision assistant that answers only from the DSE ICT curriculum and teacher notes could reduce the risk of students learning from confident but wrong AI answers. Generative media tools could help teachers produce illustrations and short explanations, as long as the content is checked and labelled. In my own lessons, I would ask students to keep a short log of the prompts they use and to mark which AI answers they checked against a source.

There are also limits that matter in schools. Students’ personal data must be protected, not every student has equal access to paid AI tools, and over-reliance on AI could weaken the skills that the DSE ICT examination and later study require. Teachers therefore need clear classroom rules about when AI may be used. This course has not changed my belief that AI is a tool, but it has given me concrete examples to explain that belief to students. It also connects to my longer-term interest in research on AI in education.

Part 3 – AI Solution Proposal

A syllabus-grounded revision assistant for DSE ICT

The problem

Many DSE ICT students revise outside class with general-purpose chatbots, as my empathy map (Artefact 2) suggests. These tools answer fluently, but large language models can produce hallucinated content: text that is nonsensical or not faithful to the source it is based on (Ji et al., 2023). For exam revision the risk is specific. An answer may be wrong, or correct but outside the current syllabus, which was revised for students starting Secondary 4 in 2022/23 (Curriculum Development Council & Hong Kong Examinations and Assessment Authority, 2021). Teachers cannot answer every question after school, so students are left to judge answers they cannot yet check.

Proposed technique and architecture

The assistant combines retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) with a simple agentic workflow (Artefact 3). RAG retrieves relevant passages from a fixed knowledge base and passes them to the model, so answers are grounded in that material, and the knowledge can be updated or inspected without retraining (Lewis et al., 2020). Here, the knowledge base holds the ICT Curriculum and Assessment Guide and the teacher’s own notes. An agent built on a large language model (e.g., Gemini) first identifies the DSE topic of a question, then chooses one of three tools: the syllabus retriever, a practice-question generator, or an answer checker that uses the teacher’s marking points. Every reply shows its source. If nothing relevant is retrieved, a guardrail replies “Not in the notes. Ask your teacher.” and flags the question for the teacher. A prototype could be built in Dify, whose knowledge base retrieves the most relevant passages for each question and passes them to the model (Dify, n.d.). Design thinking shaped the goal: the How-Might-We statement in Artefact 2 asks for answers students can trust, not simply faster answers.

Ethical, social and sustainability evaluation

  • Accuracy: grounding reduces hallucination but does not remove it, so sources are always shown and the teacher reviews flagged questions.
  • Privacy: question logs could reveal a student’s weaknesses. Under the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), personal data should be collected only for a purpose directly related to the school’s functions, and no more than that purpose needs, so the teacher sees topic summaries without names.
  • Social: a free school web app avoids a gap between students who can and cannot pay for premium AI tools. Over-reliance remains a risk, so the practice tool asks students to attempt an answer before seeing feedback.
  • Sustainability: general-purpose generative models use far more energy per task than task-specific systems (Luccioni et al., 2024). A smaller model, short retrieved passages and cached answers to common questions keep energy use and cost low.

Comparison with an alternative

The main alternative is letting students use a general chatbot directly. Its advantages are no setup, coverage of any topic and constant availability. Its disadvantages are no link to the DSE syllabus, no sources for students to check, and no way for teachers to see common misunderstandings. Fine-tuning a model on teacher notes with LoRA would also be possible, but it needs training data and retraining whenever the notes change, while RAG updates as soon as a teacher uploads a new file. The proposed assistant trades breadth for trust: it answers fewer questions, but every answer can be checked.
